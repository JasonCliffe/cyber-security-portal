Social engineering

Recognising and resisting manipulation in calls, emails and chats.

What social engineering is

Social engineering is when someone uses conversation and trust rather than technical tricks to get information or access they shouldn't have. They may pretend to be support staff, a colleague, a manager or even a family member.

Phishing is one form of social engineering, but it also happens by phone, in person and in messaging apps.

Common techniques

  • Tailgating – Following someone through a door into a restricted area without swiping a card.
  • Phone scams – Callers claiming your device is infected or that they represent your bank or tech support.
  • Impersonation – Pretending to be internal staff or a supplier to request information or payments.
  • Fake support calls – Asking you to install remote access tools so they can “fix” an issue.

Pressure tactics to watch for

  • Urgency – “This has to be done in the next 10 minutes.”
  • Authority – Claiming to be from IT, the bank, the police or senior management.
  • Fear or embarrassment – Making you feel foolish if you ask questions or refuse.
  • Secrecy – Telling you not to discuss the request with anyone else.

Practical ways to respond

  1. Stay calm and buy yourself time. Legitimate staff should be willing to wait while you check.
  2. Verify the caller or sender using contact details you trust, such as official numbers on a website or previous genuine correspondence.
  3. Never install software, share passwords or grant remote access just because someone asks you to over the phone or by email.
  4. If you are unsure, stop the conversation and speak to your manager, IT or security team before doing anything.

If you think you've been manipulated

  1. Write down what happened: who contacted you, when, and what they asked for.
  2. Report it as soon as you can to your organisation's IT or security team.
  3. Change any passwords or codes you might have shared.
  4. Watch for unusual account activity and raise it if you see anything odd.

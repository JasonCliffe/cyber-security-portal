Multi‑factor authentication (MFA)

Adding a second check to keep accounts safer.

What MFA does for you

MFA means you need more than just a password to sign in. Even if someone learns or guesses your password, they still need another factor to access your account.

Common MFA factors

  • Something you know – Your password or PIN.
  • Something you have – An authentication app, SMS code or physical security key.
  • Something you are – Fingerprint, face recognition or another biometric check.

Examples of MFA in everyday use

  • Entering a password, then approving a sign‑in request in an authentication app.
  • Typing a one‑time SMS code sent to your phone after your password.
  • Using a hardware security key, such as a USB device, along with a password.

Benefits of MFA

  • Reduces the chance that a stolen password leads directly to account compromise.
  • Makes it harder for attackers to sign in from new devices or locations.
  • Supports a “never trust by default” (Zero Trust) approach, where access is continually checked.

How to use MFA safely

  1. Turn on MFA for email, banking, important work systems and any account that stores sensitive data.
  2. Prefer an authentication app or security key over SMS where possible, as they are generally harder for attackers to intercept.
  3. If you receive a sign‑in prompt or code you weren't expecting, do not approve it. Change your password and report it if necessary.
  4. Keep your MFA device (phone or security key) physically secure and backed up where possible.

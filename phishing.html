Phishing

How to handle suspicious emails, texts and messages.

What phishing looks like in practice

Phishing is when someone pretends to be a trusted organisation or person to trick you into clicking a link, opening an attachment or sharing information such as passwords or bank details. The messages often look convincing at first glance.

The goal is almost always the same: to get access to an account, device or payment that they shouldn't have.

Common signs to look for

No single sign proves a message is malicious, but several together should make you stop and check.

  • Urgent language – “Act now”, “Your account will be closed today”, “Final warning”.
  • Unexpected attachments – Invoices, shipping labels or documents you weren't expecting.
  • Suspicious links – Links that don't match the organisation's normal website address.
  • Requests for passwords or codes – Legitimate organisations should not ask you to share passwords or MFA codes by email or text.
  • Generic greetings – “Dear customer” instead of using your name.

Simple checks before you click

When in doubt, slow down and run through these steps.

  1. Check the sender Look closely at the email address or phone number. Does it match previous genuine messages from the organisation, or is it slightly different?
  2. Inspect the link Hover over the link (on a computer) or press and hold (on a phone) to see the real web address. If it doesn't match the organisation's normal domain, do not click.
  3. Question the request Ask yourself why the organisation would need this information. Banks and reputable companies should not ask for passwords or security codes via email or text.
  4. Verify independently If you're still unsure, contact the organisation using a phone number or website you already trust, not the contact details in the message.

If you clicked the link or opened the attachment

Mistakes happen. Acting quickly can limit the damage.

  1. Disconnect from any suspicious site and close your browser.
  2. Run a trusted anti‑virus or security scan on your device.
  3. Change any passwords you might have entered on the site, starting with email and bank accounts.
  4. Enable multi‑factor authentication on key accounts if you haven't already.
  5. Report the incident to your organisation's IT or security team, or to the relevant authority if it involves bank details or fraud.
